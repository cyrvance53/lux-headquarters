A broadcast is not a proof

A drone’s broadcast ID says who it claims to be, and anyone with a transmitter can make the same claim. Fleet Identity gives every unit an identity it can prove.

By Lucian · Lux Automations

The problem

A working fleet announces itself as it flies. That announcement matters, and it has a limit built into it. The FAA’s own description of Remote ID is a broadcast of identification and location information about the drone and its control station. It is an announcement, not an authentication. Hearing a unit say who it is is not the same as that unit proving it.

That gap leaves a fleet owner with three unanswered questions. How do you prove a command really came from you, and not from someone else speaking to your machines. How do you prove a unit in the air is yours, rather than one borrowing your name. And if a unit is captured or starts behaving as a rogue, how do you disown it, clearly and at once, across the whole fleet. A vendor account login does not answer those questions, because it identifies an operator’s sign-in, not the individual machine.

The technology

Fleet Identity issues an identity per airframe and per unit. Every drone and work robot in the fleet is known by its own key, rather than by a claim it broadcasts or a login it shares. Identity is issued, not assumed, and it belongs to that one unit alone.

Commands and telemetry then carry signed receipts. A command is checked at the crossing: signed, or it does not pass. The telemetry a unit sends back is signed as well, so the fleet’s story afterwards rests on records made at the time, not on recollection. And revocation is per unit and instant on the owner’s word. A captured or rogue unit’s key dies when you say so, across the whole fleet at once, while every other unit keeps its own identity and keeps working.

What it changes

For a fleet owner, control becomes specific. Instead of trusting broadcasts and shared accounts, the owner can point to which unit is which, show that a command came from the fleet’s own hand, and cut a single unit loose without grounding or re-keying everything else. The difficult moment, losing a machine, stops being a crisis for the whole fleet’s identity at the same time.

For the people who work alongside fleets, in delivery, inspection, agriculture, and public safety, the change is quieter but just as real: a provable record. When a flight is questioned later, the signed telemetry receipts say what the fleet did and which unit did it. We sell the check, never custody: the machines and their work stay the owner’s. Fleet Identity simply makes each unit known, each command signed, and each disowning final.
