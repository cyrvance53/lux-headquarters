Strangers hiring strangers

A client fears paying for work that never comes, and a freelancer fears delivering work that is never paid for. Verified Escrow checks both sides first, and holds the release to the rules.

By Lucian · Lux Automations

The problem

A great deal of honest work begins between people who have never met. A small business hires a freelancer it found online. A freelancer takes on a client in another city, or another country. Neither side is doing anything wrong, and both sides are carrying a risk the other cannot see. The client is asked to pay before the work exists. The freelancer is asked to do the work before the pay exists.

Hope fills the gap, and hope is easy to imitate. Fake-job and fake-buyer schemes are a standing category inside the $16.6 billion of reported cybercrime losses for 2024, counted by the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center. Marketplaces answer the risk with their own trust systems, but those systems belong to the platform, with its fees and its rules, and they do little for the work that happens off-platform or across platforms.

The technology

Verified Escrow starts before the job does, with identity. Both parties are checked against issued keys before anything begins, so each side knows the other is a real, verified party rather than a name typed into a form. That check is Amos doing its ordinary work: who is knocking, and can they prove it.

Then the release waits for the work. The money is held under the switch until delivery is accepted, and acceptance is a signed receipt, not a claim made in a message thread. If the work was delivered and accepted, the record says so. If it was not, the record says that too. Behaviour carries consequences on both sides: either party can be revoked for it. To be plain about the honest limit, Verified Escrow is in pilot, and holding other people’s money carries regulatory weight, so this door opens fully only with licensed partners in place.

What it changes

For the client, paying stops being a leap. The person being hired has been verified, and the release is tied to accepted delivery rather than to a date or a promise. For the freelancer, starting work stops being a leap in the other direction. The client has been verified too, and the terms of release were set before the work began, not argued about after it was delivered.

For both sides, a disagreement changes shape. Instead of two accounts of what was promised and what arrived, there is a signed acceptance, or the absence of one, written at the time. We sell the check, never custody: the verification, the mandate, and the receipt are our part. The holding itself belongs with licensed partners, and the work and the money remain the parties’ own.
